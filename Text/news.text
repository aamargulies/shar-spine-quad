## The honest one-line summary

A small, side-indexed spine recovers ~15–25% warm and 2–3× cold at
n=4096 across six wildly different uarchs. That's the headline. The
compile-time n=4096 unroll is a shipping detail. The "Shar beats outer
spine" result is a real but narrow correction to our own prediction, not
a refutation of anything in the literature.

## The spine is the real contribution

Lemire's original article has: quaternary interpolation over blocks +
SIMD leaf compare. The descent reads probe keys directly from `carr`,
so each probe is a dependent load on an arbitrary cache line — cold-cache
pointer chase.

This project adds: a packed side index (`cardinality/gap` u16s, 256 B at
n=4096 gap=32, 128 B at n=4096 gap=64) that the descent reads from
instead. Cold-cache access pattern flips from "scattered dependent loads"
to "sequential cache-line streaming," which HW streamers handle for free.

Cold-cache results at n=4096: Pi 5 ~2×, M4 ~3×, SKX/EMR/GV4 ~2–3×. Not
tuning — a structural change to the memory access pattern.

> Correction 2026-10-11: these are within-host ratios from bench.cpp's
> "cold" column, which had no cache flush before 2026-10-11: it walked
> the arrays in allocation order with the targets the previous algorithm
> had just used,
> and the ~35 MB footprint fits the LLC of every host except the Pi 5.
> The ratios stand as measured but describe LLC-hit, not DRAM-cold,
> behaviour on five of the six hosts, and cross-host "cold" comparisons
> compare cache levels, not cores (Text/corrections_2026-10.md §5).

Why this is reportable:
  - Six-host cross-validation. Same 2–3× cold win on four different
    uarch families (A76, Apple-silicon, Intel server, Neoverse V2).
    Stronger evidence than any single-host blog post.
  - Cold-cache numbers at all. Most sorted-search microbenchmarks report
    warm-only, where the spine's advantage shrinks. The warm/cold gap on
    Roaring-sized arrays is exactly where real workloads live, and it's
    the regime Lemire's article doesn't address.
  - Roaring specifically. CRoaring ships with galloping/binary search on
    array containers; nobody appears to have proposed a side spine there.
    Concrete drop-in improvement for an actually-used library.

Where I'd temper the claim:
  - The spine as an idea isn't novel. It's the top level of a B-tree /
    cache-oblivious staircase / any sparse index over a sorted array.
    What's new is applying it to the 4096-element-cap Roaring container
    and measuring the cold/warm tradeoff across modern uarchs.
  - Additive to Lemire, not a refutation. Correct headline is "Lemire
    + spine," not "Lemire was wrong."


## Warm is the production regime, and the warm numbers still hold

Most production Roaring traffic hits warm containers (hot working sets,
repeated contains/union operations on the same key space). So the warm
numbers are the ones that matter for the sell; cold is the contingency
story for first-touch paths and cache-thrash regimes (e.g. set-union
scans across many containers).

## Absolute EMR headline at n=4096

Two ways to read it, and the honest story needs both.

Incremental: tuned-Intel without vs. with spine (same hardware path):
  - Warm: 19.5  -> 14.02  =  -28% / -5.5 ns
  - Cold: 64.9  -> 25.87  =  -60% / -39 ns / 2.51x faster

Versus Lemire's reference (the SSE2 quaternary):
  - Warm: 13.8  -> 14.02  =  essentially tied
  - Cold: 71.0  -> 25.87  =  2.74x faster / -45 ns

So EMR at n=4096 is "cold-cache 2.7x over Lemire, warm-cache a wash."
That actually strengthens the spine-is-the-real-news framing: the spine
solves a cold-memory problem, and EMR's warm path was already near the
AVX-512 issue limit where there's nothing left to win.

> Correction 2026-10-11: the 14.02 / 25.87 cells are the retired
> unroll's. The shipped hybrid is 6.50 warm / 24.36 cold at n=4096

> -34.5% cold vs the general-n spine; -54.8% warm vs the unroll), so EMR
> is not "warm-cache a wash" against the 13.8 ns reference. The cold
> numbers are from bench.cpp's then-unflushed cold column
> (Text/corrections_2026-10.md §5).

Separately: the `simd_quad_intel` warm regression vs. the SSE2 reference
at 13.8 -> 19.5 is itself interesting — the zmm single-compare
block-check pays a fixed cost that the warm SSE2 path doesn't. Spine +
unroll land us back at parity warm and 2.7x ahead cold. Net: the AVX-512

## The Shar-outer result, in proportion

Claim: "is it fair to say Lemire's SIMD article has been refuted /
replaced by Shar?" Answer: no, that conflates two search levels.

Lemire's article is about searching inside one sorted container. The
winning shape on every host in this project is still what Lemire
proposed: quaternary descent + SIMD block-compare at the bottom. The
SIMD block-check (NEON vceqq OR-tree, AVX2 cmpeq+movemask, or EMR's
zmm+kortest) is load-bearing on all six hosts — nothing has replaced it.

Shar is about the *outer* search across 512 container keys in the
two-level RoaringSet — a problem Lemire's article doesn't address. Shar
beats the outer spine because the K=512 key array is 1–8 cache lines
(too short for any stride prefetcher to exploit), so a branchless cmov
chain of ~9 independent scalar loads beats a dependent pointer chase.
That calculus changes at the inner level: n=4096 = 64 lines, the spine
streams cleanly, and Shar isn't competitive there.

> Correction 2026-10-11: Shar's outer is a serial cmov chain (load ->
> compare -> csel -> load, each address depending on the previous
> select), not ~9 independent loads; it wins hot because it has no
> branch mispredicts and no side structure. On cold data the branchy
> std::lower_bound outer (A, C) speculates ahead across mispredicted
> branches, which is why C -- not Shar -- is the cold winner on EMR and
> GV4 (5/5 in both cold modes; Text/corrections_2026-10.md §5, §2.1).

What was actually refuted is narrower: our prediction that the outer
spine would win on Intel because of LLC streamers. It didn't — not on
SKX, not on EMR. Scalar-vs-scalar argument about which descent shape
fits a short key array, not about SIMD.

Honest endgame: **Shar outside + Lemire-style SIMD-quad inside**. The
two compose; they don't compete. The only thing that lost is the
assumption that the same descent shape works at both levels.

> Correction 2026-10-11: hot-only. Paired 4-mode data
> ({host}_runs/twolevel_paired.txt, Text/corrections_2026-10.md section
> 2.2): the outer spine (D) beats Shar (F) in cold_ser on both Intel
> hosts, SKX +16.9% (F faster 0/5) and EMR +17.7% (0/5); the cold-best
> variant is C or D (sections 2.1–2.3), so "Shar outside + quad inside"
> is the hot-mode ship only.


## How was the Shar-outer win missed?

Honest answer: "missed by everyone" overstates it. The surprise is
narrower than it looks.

Shar itself isn't unknown. Branchless binary search via cmov + step-
halving is standard fare in the benchmark-oriented corner — probablydance
2023, Abseil-style work, Paul Khuong's writeups, game-engine lower_bound
benchmarks. Anyone who has microbenchmarked sorted-array lookup on a
modern OoO core has probably landed on it. The 2026 "discovery" isn't
the algorithm.

What's new here is narrow and specific to this project:

  1. Measuring it in the Roaring outer-level context. Real CRoaring uses
     plain binary/galloping search on container keys, not a spine.
     Nobody in Roaring-land was comparing "outer spine" to Shar because
     nobody was proposing an outer spine. *We* proposed the outer spine
     here, discovered it didn't beat Shar, and that's the result. It's
     less "Shar won" and more "our outer-spine idea lost to the obvious
     baseline."

  2. The refuted prediction was ours. The "Intel LLC streamers will love
     a multi-line outer spine" hypothesis was plausible at the
     whiteboard and specifically wrong because K=512 * 2 B = 1 KB is
     just too short for any stride prefetcher to see enough lines to
     engage. That's the real learning, and it's about prefetcher working
     sets, not about Shar.

  3. Six-host convergence. Cross-host agreement (Pi 5 / M1 / M4 / SKX /
     EMR / GV4) on the same winner is unusual; most blog-post benchmarks
     are single-host. But the result each host produces individually
     wouldn't surprise anyone who runs their own numbers.

> Correction 2026-10-11: the convergence is hot-only. Paired per-run

> and loses cold_ser to B, D and E. The Pi 5 ship recommendation is D

Why the SIMD-quad framing persists in the literature: Lemire's article
is about the inner-container problem, which *is* genuinely SIMD-shaped.
Papers cluster around the interesting vector-instruction story; "the
512-entry key array on top was fine with branchless binary search" isn't
a paper. That's not something being missed — it's a problem nobody
framed.

Real contribution on the outer level: a *local* result about a specific
two-level layout with a specific small K, not a general refutation
anyone overlooked. Honest headline: "our outer-spine idea didn't pan
out; the plain branchless baseline is what you should ship." Less
exciting title, more accurate.

> or D (sections 2.1–2.3).


## What to sell, in priority order

1. **Spine + compile-time n=4096 beats Lemire at n=4096.** 15–42% warm
   on the older/narrower hosts; 2–3x cold across all six. This is the
   blog-post-worthy result. Six-host validation gives it weight no
   single-host benchmark would have.

> Correction 2026-10-11: the warm percentages are unroll-era; with the
> hybrid the compile-time n=4096 kernel is below the reference on all
> six hosts (regenerated bench_table.txt, warm n=4096: 15.98 vs 31.1
> Pi 5, 3.71 vs 6.3 M4 Max, 6.05 vs 9.2 M1 Pro, 18.92 vs 33.0 SKX, 6.50
> vs 13.8 EMR, 7.00 vs 15.2 GV4). "2-3x cold" is bench.cpp's then-unflushed

> F-vs-Lemire result holds at n=4096 (F faster in all 24 host x mode

> container sizes: Lemire's variant beats F in 5/5 runs at n=48-62 and
> n=81-124 hot_bat and at n=1-7 and n=64-95 cold_ser on the M4 Max

2. **Six-host uarch-dependent tuning catalog.** Gap tracks cache line,
   not SIMD width. Speculative prefetch helps narrow-OoO (A76) and
   downclocked-scalar (SKX), hurts wide-OoO (M-series, EMR, GV4).
   Apple-silicon gap=64 is unique. These are concrete, portable lessons
   that CRoaring and similar libraries could apply.
